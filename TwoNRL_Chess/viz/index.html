Train a network to break the rules, then flip its sign

A self-building network is handed a chessboard and none of the rules. It proposes one of 4096 from–to pairs, the board refuses or accepts, and it proposes again until something sticks. Then it is trained to fail on purpose — and negated.

The negative phase goes the wrong way on purpose

Refusals per move on the held-out positions, averaged over three seeds. The inverting arms climb through phase 1 — that is the network being driven toward its own failures at the full learning rate — and drop vertically at the flip. Log scale, because the range is two orders of magnitude.

Arms

Where the inversion helps, and where it hurts

The operator is neutral. What you point it at is everything.

The same sign flip, applied to four networks that differ only in what phase 1 was trained toward. Aim it at moves the board refused and it pays sevenfold. Aim it at a legal move — Stockfish's worst, or one drawn at random — and phase 1 teaches legality by accident, so the flip throws it away.

Head to head

The trained networks play each other

Shared openings with the colours swapped, so the pairing cancels the opening draw. A common opponent and a random legal mover are there because a head-to-head between two weak players can be a rock-paper-scissors artefact.

Against the paper's own predictions

What held, and what did not

Stockfish 16 plays at Skill Level 20 — no handicap — and grades at full strength; python-chess supplies the rules and refuses illegal moves. Engines are depth-limited, never time-limited, so the numbers do not move when the machine is busy. Refusals per move is a property of the network and the rules, not of the opponent: no engine setting can change it.
